The same failure, nine times,
in different clothing.

Every time, the model produced the statistically likely answer.

Every time, it broke where the binary departed from likely — the arbitrary rule, the padding, the decoy, the custom constant, the exact offset.

Every time, it was caught with evidence a student could read because they'd built the skill by hand.

One law, observed nine times.
And you derived it yourselves.

Where these skills go

  • Vulnerability research — bugs in software you have no source to. The Lecture 4 return-address moment was a keyhole into this
  • Malware analysis — understanding hostile code, in isolated monitored environments
  • Firmware / embedded / IoT — AURA-7's whole premise. No symbols, odd architectures, physical devices
  • Interoperability & preservation — undocumented formats and protocols; keeping old software alive. Often the most clearly lawful face
  • Anti-cheat, DRM, licensing — the AURA-7 thread, as an industry, on both sides
  • Compliance & audit — verifying a vendor's binary does what they claim

The law, and the ethics

Everything this semester was done to binaries this course made, for learning. That is the safe square. Outside it, the ground is real.

  • It varies by jurisdiction. EU: interoperability permitted under conditions. US: DMCA restricts circumvention, with shifting research/interop exceptions. And the licence you clicked can forbid what statute allows. Not legal advice — a map of the landmines.
  • Responsible disclosure. Find a real bug? Tell the vendor, give them time, coordinate. A professional norm
  • The bright line. A lab binary is not someone's production server. Skill is not permission.

The left is acceleration.

The person who cannot read a stack frame cannot safely use either column.

The race — 75 minutes

A binary nobody has seen

Table A

No AI. Tools, notes, your own reading.

Table B

AI only. Relay its instructions, type what it says. No independent reasoning. You are its hands.

Table C

Human + AI. Anything goes. The realistic mode.

Recover the licence algorithm, produce one valid key. First verified key wins; best hypothesis log wins if nobody finishes.

Table B's constraint is artificial
on purpose.

It isolates one question: what does pure delegation, with no independent judgement behind it, actually get you?

You won't take the answer on faith. You'll watch it happen at your own table.

The prediction: Table B has the fastest first ten minutes in the room — then hits a wall it cannot climb, because climbing it needs exactly the thing that table gave up. Remember how that feels.

Debrief — 35 minutes · the actual point

Not who won. How each table failed.

Table B

Fastest start. Stalled — wrong algorithm, or narrated the decoy — and, forbidden to reason, re-prompted into the same wrong answer. Couldn't tell it was wrong.

Table A

Slow, solid. Every step understood, no time lost to misplaced trust. No acceleration on the dull parts.

Table C

Fast and recovered at the wall — the same wall — because they could read the evidence and knew to overrule.

Table C is not "AI plus vibes". It's "AI plus someone who can catch it". The catching is the entire skill.

Run Table B's questions first: where did you get stuck? what did you do when it was wrong? could you tell? If AI-only unexpectedly finishes, do NOT fake a stall — run the honest "the wall moved" debrief. Course credibility is honesty about the tools.

The tools will keep improving

The wall will move. The class of binary that defeats pure delegation will get narrower and stranger.

But the method for finding the wall does not change. And the ability to read the evidence when you reach it does not go out of date.

When the tool is right, that ability lets you move fast.
When it's wrong, it's the only thing between you and a confident, plausible, wrong answer.

That is the job now.

Not doing the reverse engineering by hand — directing the machine that does most of it, and knowing, from evidence, when it is lying.

And you can only direct
what you understand.
